# How policy failed the public, which checks did not fire, and what would make them fire

A structural analysis, written after the AI/SI work in this session, on the question: *where did
American public policy fail the public, which constitutional checks were supposed to catch it,
and what would have to change for the promises of the Declaration and the guarantees of the
Constitution — federal and in all fifty states — to be met?*

**Status of this document.** It is analysis, not legal advice, and not a pleading. Constitutional
citations below are offered as **pointers from memory (tier C)**; a filing-grade version must pull
each one from the reporter and check the holding against the current court's treatment. Where I am
inferring rather than citing, the sentence says so. No case is cited that I could not name and
describe; nothing is invented to fill a gap.

---

## 1. First, separate two kinds of failure

**Process failure** — a check that exists did not operate: no hearing, no finding, no recorded
reason, no report acted on. These are diagnosable and fixable, and they are the ones this document
is about.

**Outcome failure** — a decision was taken properly and produced a bad result. Some of this is
constitutional design working: veto gates, federalism, supermajorities and staggered elections
were *meant* to make action slow and reversible. Calling every bad outcome a constitutional
failure is how the diagnosis gets lost.

The bridge between them is the one this session kept hitting: **a system cannot be held to account
for what it does not measure.** A dated claim with no source tier, a byte figure nobody measured, a
"verified" with no receipt — at national scale those are the same defect as a procurement
justification that asserts a capability and never tests it. Failure of measurement is failure of
accountability before it is failure of policy.

---

## 2. Seven ways policy failed, stated as mechanisms

1. **Delegation without a standard.** Congress writes a goal, hands rule-making to an agency, and
   keeps neither the expertise nor the blame. The public then argues with an agency that has no
   electoral mandate and a legislature that has no operational responsibility.
2. **Emergency as the ordinary.** Statute-based emergencies (national emergency declarations,
   sanctions and tariff authorities, public-health powers) are renewable and rarely terminated,
   so temporary authority becomes the base case and the legislative check becomes a vote nobody
   is forced to take.
3. **The budget as a hostage.** Continuing resolutions, omnibus packages and debt-limit brinkmanship
   convert the power of the purse from a check into a periodic crisis, and crisis is the worst
   possible condition for oversight.
4. **Information asymmetry as a business model.** For policy that depends on technical claims —
   compute, energy, medicine, finance — the regulator knows less than the regulated, and nothing
   compels the artefact that would close the gap.
5. **Concentrated benefit, diffuse cost.** A plant, a tariff, a data centre, a tax provision: the
   gain is legible and organised, the loss is spread across people who never learn they paid it.
   This is the mechanism behind most "policy failed the public" stories, and no clause bans it.
6. **Attribution collapse.** Voters cannot tell who did what, because authority is split across
   agencies, states, private contractors and international bodies, so the sanction that elections
   are supposed to apply has no target.
7. **Local news and civic infrastructure decay.** The check that operates daily — a reporter asking
   a clerk for a document — has been defunded in most of the country, and no constitutional
   provision requires anyone to fund it.

## 3. Which checks did not fire

| check | text / source | why it did not fire | what would make it fire |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Legislative supremacy** | Art. I §1; non-delegation doctrine last enforced seriously in *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry* (1935) | Congress prefers delegation: it delivers programmes without owning failures | a statutory standard for major rules (a codified "major questions" test, per *West Virginia v. EPA*, 2022) and a vote on each |
| **Appropriations** | Art. I §9 §7 | CRs and omnibus bundles replace twelve bills; shutdowns are the leverage | automatic continuing resolution at last enacted level, so a shutdown costs nobody a hostage |
| **Advice and consent** | Art. II §2 | vacancy strategy: leave offices empty rather than lose a vote | statutory deadlines with default appointments and a Senate duty to act |
| **Take care** | Art. II §3 | enforcement discretion is unreviewable in practice; prosecutorial priorities move silently | published enforcement policy with reasons, and a duty to report non-enforcement |
| **Judicial review** | *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) | the courthouse door is shut before the merits: standing (*Lujan*, 1992), mootness, arbitration clauses (*AT&T v. Concepcion*, 2011), qualified immunity (*Pierson v. Ray*, 1967) | statutory causes of action for diffuse harms (algorithmic, environmental, informational); limits on pre-dispute arbitration for civil-rights claims |
| **Deference and its correction** | *Chevron* (1984), overruled by *Loper Bright* (2024) | for forty years courts deferred to the agency; now the correction risks courts substituting their own policy | clear-statement rules plus agency reason-giving, so the record — not the judge's preference — decides |
| **Separation of powers inside Congress** | *INS v. Chadha* (1983); *Bowsher v. Synar* (1986); *Clinton v. City of New York* (1998) | these fired and struck the mechanisms down; Congress then stopped building accountable shortcuts and delegated instead | Article I process reform (committee markup, recorded amendments) rather than new shortcuts |
| **Political accountability of districting** | *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019) | held partisan gerrymandering a political question — the federal check removed itself | independent commissions and state-constitutional provisions (several states already have them) |
| **Campaign finance disclosure** | *Buckley* (1976); *Citizens United* (2010) | independent expenditure is protected speech; disclosure is under-enforced | real-time disclosure, donor identity thresholds, and public financing where a state's constitution allows |
| **Fiscal federalism** | *South Dakota v. Dole* (1987); 10th Amendment | conditional grants let Washington set state policy while states carry the political cost | no preemption and no condition without a stated constitutional hook and full cost accounting |
| **The jury as a check** | 6th and 7th Amendments | plea bargaining resolves ~97% of criminal cases; trials are rare, so the citizen check almost never convenes | discovery and bail reform that make trial survivable, and civil jury access where arbitration has displaced it |
| **Amendment as error correction** | Art. V | two-thirds/two-thirds/three-quarters is close to unusable in a polarised system | a serious Article V risk assessment (a convention is not a safe tool); absent that, state constitutions become the working correction |
| **Presidential accountability** | Art. II; *Trump v. United States* (2024) | the Court recognised broad immunity for official acts, narrowing the criminal check | congressional oversight with enforceable subpoenas; ethics and recusal statutes with independent enforcement |
| **Oath and ethics enforcement** | Art. VI; ethics statutes | enforcement is internal, under-resourced, and vacancies go unfilled | independent enforcement with a duty to publish findings, and protection for inspectors general |

**Two honest notes on the table.** First, several rows describe checks that *did* fire and were
then removed or narrowed by later choices — the fix is legislative, not judicial. Second, a check
that returns a political answer (Rucho) has not failed; it has been *assigned* to politics, which
means the remedy is at the ballot and in state law, not in a courtroom.

## 4. The fifty-state layer, which is where the real floor now sits

State constitutions are the most under-used rights instruments in the country: they can protect
more than the federal Constitution, and they are amended far more often. Four mechanisms matter:

- **Education clauses.** Almost every state constitution guarantees a system of public education;
  courts have found funding systems unconstitutional in Kentucky (*Rose*, 1989), Texas (*Edgewood*,
  1989), New Jersey (*Abbott*), Washington (*McCleary*, 2012), Kansas (*Gannon*), Ohio (*DeRolph*,
  1997) and California (*Serrano*, 1971). The pattern is the finding: **the right is declared, the
  remedy is deferred for decades** — the check fires and nothing happens.
- **Environmental rights amendments.** Montana (Art. IX §1), Pennsylvania (Art. I §27, 1971) and
  New York (2021) create enforceable duties that the federal Constitution lacks; *Held v. Montana*
  (2023, affirmed 2024) is the current test of whether they bite.
- **Preemption of cities.** States routinely forbid municipal minimum wages, sick leave, broadband
  and plastic-bag rules. That is a check on local democracy exercised *by* a state, and it is
  almost never counted in "who failed the public".
- **Judicial selection.** Elected state supreme courts raise a capture risk that appointed federal
  courts do not, which is why the state layer is both the best hope and the softest target.

## 5. What would actually resolve it, ordered by Article

1. **Art. I — vote on the rule.** Major policy needs a statute with a standard and a recorded vote;
   no agency rule without a statutory anchor. Test: can a citizen name the clause that authorised
   the rule?
2. **Art. I — a budget that is a check.** Automatic CR at the prior level; no shutdown leverage.
3. **Art. II — publish the legal basis.** Every emergency action names its statute, its expiry and
   its renewal vote. Test: does the declaration expire without a recorded decision?
4. **Art. II — receipts in procurement.** A capability claim without a certificate is a contract
   defect (this is HR-2/HR-6 from the SI bible, and F4/F5 from the pre-commit gate, at national
   scale).
5. **Art. III — open the door.** Statutory causes of action for diffuse harms; arbitration limits
   for civil-rights claims; a merits decision, not a standing dismissal.
6. **Art. IV/VI — a federalism floor.** No preemption without a constitutional hook; states free to
   exceed federal protection.
7. **Art. V — stop pretending it is available.** Either make the amendment path usable or treat
   state constitutions as the correction mechanism, deliberately.
8. **Art. VI/1st — fund the daily check.** Local news and public-records enforcement are
   infrastructure, not charity.

## 6. What the Declaration supplies that the Constitution does not

The Declaration is not self-executing and no court enforces "the pursuit of happiness". What it
supplies is the **standard the Constitution is measured against**: equality as a premise rather
than a grant; rights that precede government ("unalienable"); consent as the source of authority;
and the right of the people "to alter or to abolish" a government that becomes destructive of those
ends. Its practical use is diagnostic — a policy that is lawful but leaves a class of people
outside the promise has failed the Declaration even when it passes the Constitution.

---

## 7. What this document does not establish

It does not establish that any particular programme was unlawful; it does not adjudicate any
pending matter; and it does not claim the courts are the correct forum for every failure — several
remedies named here are legislative by design. The case citations are pointers to be verified
before use. The one claim I will defend without qualification is the one the rest of this session
kept demonstrating: **a check that leaves no record cannot fire, and a claim that carries no
artefact cannot be reviewed.**
