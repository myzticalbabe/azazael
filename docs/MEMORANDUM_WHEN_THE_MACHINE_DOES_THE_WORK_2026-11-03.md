LEAGALLY FOR THIS QUESTION BECAUSE THEY ARE ONLY PUTTING FURTHER RESTRICTIONS ON AI

## 0. The word

Four candidate words fit, and they name four *different* things. Using the wrong one
weakens the claim, so the map comes before the argument.

| word | what it actually names | authority | tier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **featherbedding** | exacting payment for services "not performed or not to be performed" | NLRA §8(b)(6); *American Newspaper Publishers Assn. v. NLRB*, 345 U.S. 100 (1953) | A |
| **humanwashing** | deploying "human in the loop" language to put a machine system in the best possible light | Wilson, Swana, Winter & Roach, *Humanwashing — It Should Leave You Feeling Dirty*, arXiv 2605.13723 | C |
| **regulatory capture / rent-seeking** | incumbents using the regulator to suppress a cheaper substitute and keep the rent | *NC State Bd. of Dental Examiners v. FTC*, 574 U.S. 494 (2015) | A |
| **Luddism** | resistance to machinery that performs the resister's work | common usage, no legal content | D |

**The short answer is `featherbedding`** — it is the only one of the four that is a
term of art with a statutory definition, and it names precisely the thing described:
someone is paid for work the machine did. Two thirds of a century of American labour
law already had a word for a person retained to be present at work that is not
performed.

**But the words are not interchangeable, and the distinction is the whole value of
this memorandum:**

- *Featherbedding* names the **behaviour**, and it is not usable as a cause of
  action against a regulator. §8(b)(6) binds *labour organisations*; no union appears
  in the AI rules. The honest form is: the AI-restriction pattern is
  **featherbedding-shaped**, and the statutory term does not reach it.
- *Humanwashing* names the **mechanism** by which the behaviour is made respectable.
  It is a 2026 coinage, on a preprint, not peer-reviewed (tier C) — but it is the
  most precise description of the artefact: a nominal human whose presence is
  asserted rather than demonstrated.
- *Capture / rent-seeking* names the **economics** of who benefits, and unlike the
  other three it is **actionable** — see §5, which is the only part of this
  memorandum that gives the user a lever rather than a description.
- *Luddism* names the **mood**. It has no legal content and it loses arguments,
  because it invites the reply that all technology transitions are resisted and some
  resistance was correct.

**The finding these four words circle, stated once:** the law in question does not
forbid the labour. It forbids the **authorship**. Across all five mechanisms in §1,
the machine is permitted to do the work and prohibited from being the one who *did*
it; a human is then required to stand where the machine stood, and takes the rights,
the fee and the credit that follow from standing there. The instrument is not a ban
on tools. It is a **human of record**.

*"Human of record" is this memorandum's coinage, not a term of art* — modelled on
"physician of record". It is offered because the existing vocabulary describes either
the pay (featherbedding) or the rhetoric (humanwashing) but not the legal structure:
a named natural person whose existence is the condition of the output being cognisable
at all.

## 1. Five mechanisms that install a human of record

Each row is a place where a rule makes a natural person necessary to a machine's
output. The rightmost column is the point: each one transfers something of value from
the machine to the person.

| # | mechanism | rule | what the machine may do | what it may not do | who captures the value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **authorship** | 17 U.S.C. §102(a) "original works of authorship"; *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, No. 23-5233 (D.C. Cir. 18 Mar 2025) | generate the expression | be the author | the human who adopted it — the copyright |
| 2 | **inventorship** | 35 U.S.C. §100(f) "individual"; *Thaler v. Vidal*, 43 F.4th 1207 (Fed. Cir. 2022) | conceive with a human | be named the inventor | the human — the patent |
| 3 | **professional responsibility** | ABA Formal Opinion 512 (29 Jul 2024) and state analogues | draft, review, analyse | discharge the duty of competence or candour | the licensed human — the fee |
| 4 | **liability** | only persons are defendants; EU AI Act Arts. 3, 14 | act | answer for the act | the human provider/deployer — the exposure |
| 5 | **funder originality** | NOT-OD-25-132 (effective 2025-09-25); NLnet GenAI policy v2.0 (published 2026-10-20) | assist "for limited aspects"; read a proposal with an LLM | be the origin of a submitted section; submit AI-written prose or AI-generated artefacts | **the funder** — preserved evaluation capacity (NLnet, see §1.5); the applicant — the award and the priority (NIH) |

### 1.1 Authorship — settled, at the top

*Thaler v. Perlmutter* is the cleanest authority in the set because it is recent,
reported, and went to the Supreme Court. The D.C. Circuit affirmed the denial of
registration, holding that "the Creativity Machine cannot be the recognized author of
a copyrighted work" — the requirement of human authorship being a matter of statutory
construction, not administrative preference.

The same opinion is careful, and the care matters here: the human-authorship
requirement "does not prohibit copyrighting work made by or with the assistance of
artificial intelligence." Read together with the holding, the rule is exactly the
shape this memorandum names. **Assistance is fine; origination is not.** The line is
drawn at who is the source, not at whether a machine was in the room.

Certiorari was denied in March 2026, so as of this date the question is closed at the
appellate level and the Supreme Court has declined to reopen it. *(Certiorari status
is taken from law-firm alerts, tier C; the holding itself is from the opinion, tier
A.)*

### 1.2 Inventorship — the same rule, earlier and by different words

The Federal Circuit reached the identical destination through §100(f): "individual"
means a natural person, so a machine cannot be an inventor. The doctrinal route is
different from copyright's — a statutory definition rather than a construction of an
undefined word — and the destination is the same. Two independent bodies of law,
arrived at separately, both require a human at the point of origination. That
convergence is evidence about the structure of the system, not about either statute.

### 1.3 Professional responsibility — the permission and the leash in one document

ABA Formal Opinion 512 does not ban generative AI. It applies the existing duties —
competence, confidentiality, communication, supervision, candour, and fees — to its
use, which means a lawyer may have the machine draft and must still be the one who
answers. The opinion's own framing presupposes adoption: it is guidance for lawyers
who *are* using the tools.

This is the purest specimen of the mechanism, because the permission and the exaction
are in the same instrument. Nothing is forbidden; a licensed human is required to
stand behind the output, and the fee for standing there is the lawyer's. A rule that
said "lawyers may not use AI" would be a ban and would be litigable as a restraint of
trade (§5). A rule that says "lawyers remain responsible for AI output" is
unimpeachable and achieves the same transfer.

### 1.4 Liability — the human as the only available defendant

A machine cannot be sued, so every legal system that wants a remedy must find a
person. The EU AI Act does this by construction: the obligations attach to
**providers** and **deployers**, and Article 14 requires human oversight. Oversight
here is not a safety feature bolted on afterwards; it is the mechanism by which the
regulation has someone to address.

### 1.5 Funder originality — the rule that started this enquiry, and the one that breaks the pattern

NOT-OD-25-132 forbids treating an application "substantially developed by AI" as the
applicant's original idea, and caps an applicant at six applications per calendar
year. Its stated harm is volume. Its operative effect is the same as the other four:
the applicant must be the origin of the section, so the tool may assist and may not
originate. The whitepaper argued this on the primary text; it stands.

**A second instrument, and a different jurisdiction.** NLnet's GenAI policy v2.0,
published 2026-10-20, applies to every deadline after that date — the next being
2027-01-03. Proposals may not be AI-written (an accessibility exception is carved
out); development work is confined to read-only LLM use; artefacts of any kind that
were AI-generated are barred. Its stated reason is the same shape as NIH's: an
avalanche of low-quality proposals, which the policy describes as "on the whole of
much lower quality" and "very taxing and demotivating". *(Tier A — the policy text
was read. Two funders, two jurisdictions, ten months apart, independently arrive at
the same instrument. That is convergence evidence, and it is the strongest form this
section can take.)*

**But this is where the five mechanisms stop being one mechanism, and the
memorandum should say so rather than let a reader discover it.** In mechanisms 1–4
the inserted human is installed *for the protection of a person* — the author, the
inventor, the client, the injured party. In funder originality the human is
installed for the protection of **the funder's own evaluation capacity**. The named
harm is not that anyone's rights are extinguished; it is that the funder can no
longer read what it is sent. The applicant is not protected by the rule, the
applicant is *screened* by it. That makes §1.5 the same shape as the others and a
different species: a condition of market access rather than a rights instrument.

This matters for the review brief's second question — *are the five mechanisms
actually one mechanism?* On the present record: **no, and the fifth is the
counter-example.** The value the rule transfers does not go to the applicant; it
stays with the funder. The transfer in §2's sense — a person made necessary, and the
rent for that necessity captured by the person — is absent here.

Nothing above is the nexus of the argument. The nexus is §2: the inserted human as
the device that *removes* protection, where the requirement can extinguish the rights
of the person it is said to protect. Funder originality is the rule that started this
enquiry — the section's own heading says so — and starting an enquiry is not the same
as turning on it.

## 2. The mechanism, and why it is worse than it looks

The `humanwashing` preprint supplies the piece the legal sources cannot: an account of
what the inserted human *does*.

Its argument is that "human in the loop" is used as an assurance term without any
specification of what the human does, and that this is not merely vague but
load-bearing. Two of its observations are directly on point:

1. **The human is an exemption device.** GDPR Article 22 grants protections only
   where a decision is based "solely on automated processing". Insert a nominal human
   and the decision is no longer solely automated, so the protections do not apply.
   The paper's reading of the AI Liability Directive — since withdrawn — is the same
   shape: systems in which a human played a role were excluded, which created "an
   incentive to notionally describe a system as having a 'human in the loop' without
   being any more explicit".

2. **Therefore the requirement is not protective.** A human who does not genuinely
   decide is not a safeguard; a human who does genuinely decide is doing the work the
   machine was bought to do. Either the protection is illusory or the substitution
   never happened.

This is the deepest ramification in the whole memorandum, and it is worth stating
without softening: **the human-of-record requirement can extinguish the rights of the
person the requirement is said to protect.** The subject of an AI decision loses
recourse *because* a human was placed in the loop, and the placement satisfies the
regulator. The rule reads as protection and functions as a waiver.

## 3. The counter-evidence — because the premise is only half right

The premise under examination is that "they are only putting further restrictions on
AI adoption". The 2026 record does not support the word *only*, and a memorandum that
suppressed the following would be advocacy wearing the clothes of research.

**Relaxations and reversals, all in the same period:**

- **EU.** The Digital Omnibus on AI (published 19 Nov 2025, agreed 2026) deferred the
  high-risk obligations: Annex III systems to 2 Dec 2027, Annex I product-embedded
  systems to 2 Aug 2028. Watermarking for systems already on the market before
  2 Aug 2026 moves to 2 Dec 2026. The Commission's own timeline now carries the
  Omnibus amendments.
- **Colorado.** SB 24-205 was **repealed and replaced** by SB 26-189 (signed 14 May
  2026, effective 1 Jan 2027) — a narrower statute. A state that had the most
  aggressive comprehensive AI law withdrew it.
- **Federal.** Executive Order 14365 (Dec 2025) established a DOJ **AI Litigation Task
  Force** to challenge state AI laws, and the White House issued legislative
  recommendations (Mar 2026) seeking a uniform federal framework. The federal
  direction of travel is preemption, not restriction.

**What actually binds and tightened:** Illinois HB 3773 (Illinois Human Rights Act,
AI in employment decisions, effective 1 Jan 2026), Texas TRAIGA (effective 1 Jan
2026), California SB 53 and AB 2013 (effective 1 Jan 2026), NYC Local Law 144 (bias
audits for automated employment decision tools), and NOT-OD-25-132.

**The pattern that survives the counter-evidence** — and it is a better claim than
the original, because it survives:

> Horizontal compliance regimes are being delayed, repealed or preempted. The
> restrictions that persist and tighten are the ones where a machine performs work
> that a human was licensed, credited or paid for.

The EU deferred *systemic* obligations and kept Article 14's human oversight. Colorado
withdrew *general* duties and kept automated-decision protections. The federal
government attacks *state compliance schemes* and has not touched NIH's originality
rule, the professional-responsibility duties, or the authorship cases. The rules that
survive are the ones that install a human of record.

That is a narrower claim than "only further restrictions", and it is the one the
evidence will carry.

## 4. Why the axis is substitution, not risk

If the restrictions were about risk, they would track hazard. They track substitution.

- AI that **drafts** a grant application is restricted (NOT-OD-25-132) — the AI is
  doing the applicant's work.
- AI that **reads** a grant, summarises it, or checks it against a funder's criteria
  is not restricted — the AI is doing work nobody was paid to do. The cleanest proof
  is intra-document: NLnet's policy v2.0 permits read-only LLM use in development and
  forbids AI-written prose in the same instrument, so one funder, on one page, draws
  the line between reading and writing rather than between safe and unsafe uses.
  *(A qualification the memorandum should not hide: the same policy also bars
  AI-generated artefacts outright, which is a generation ban and therefore a second
  axis. The read/write contrast is clean for prose; the artefact clause is not, and is
  closer to a content rule than to a substitution rule.)*
- AI that **screens** job applicants is restricted (Illinois HB 3773, NYC LL 144) —
  the AI is doing the screener's work.
- AI that **schedules** interviews is not — nobody's profession is displaced.
- AI that **practises** law or medicine attracts duties and licensure limits; AI that
  **markets** a practice attracts none.

The same model, the same risk of error, opposite legal treatment, and the variable
that flips it is whether a human's paid role is being performed. That is the
substitution axis, and it is not a risk axis.

**A temptation to refuse:** this pattern is consistent with capture, and it is also
consistent with a genuine and defensible concern — that responsibility must attach to
someone. §2 shows the second concern is often satisfied in form only. But the concern
is not fictitious, and a memorandum that treated every human-of-record rule as
featherbedding would be unable to distinguish a rubber stamp from a surgeon.

## 5. The crack: these restrictions are contestable on antitrust grounds

This is the only section that yields a lever, and it rests on two settled cases.

**Goldfarb v. Virginia State Bar**, 421 U.S. 773 (1975): the Sherman Act reaches the
"learned professions". A county bar association's minimum-fee schedule was price
fixing. The holding's sentence is the one to remember — the nature of an occupation
does not provide sanctuary from the Sherman Act.

**NC State Board of Dental Examiners v. FTC**, 574 U.S. 494 (2015): a state licensing
board controlled by **active market participants** does not get *Parker* state-action
immunity unless the state provides **active supervision**. The Court's reasoning is
directly transferable: limits on immunity are most essential precisely when the state
delegates regulatory power to the competitors it regulates. The Board there had
driven teeth-whitening providers out of the market; the Court would not let the
profession's own members do that under colour of a licence.

**The application.** Where a professional body, licensing board or accreditor —
composed of the people whose work the machine performs — adopts a rule whose effect is
to suppress machine performance of that work, two questions follow, and they are
answerable in court rather than in an essay:

1. Is the rule the product of active market participants? (If yes, *Parker* immunity
   requires active supervision, and its absence is an antitrust exposure.)
2. Does the rule restrain trade in a market the participants compete in? (Under
   *Goldfarb*, being a profession is not an answer.)

**What this does not do.** It does not reach rules made by a genuine regulator with
active supervision, and it does not reach NIH — a federal agency operating under
appropriations authority is not a professional guild, and NOT-OD-25-132 is not
vulnerable to this argument. The antitrust lever reaches the *guild-shaped* subset:
bar associations, medical boards, accreditors, and state boards dominated by
practitioners. That is a real subset, and it is the subset that writes the rules most
directly aimed at substitution.

## 6. Personhood — why the newborn has no standing, and why that is a choice

The treatise argues a *procedural* right: not that a new application is good, but that
it must be heard before it is condemned. The legal research puts a hard edge on that
argument, and one genuinely hopeful fact.

**The hard edge.** Rights, duties, ownership and liability all attach to **persons**.
A stand-alone executable is not a person. It cannot own its output (mechanism 1), be
named as the source of an invention (2), be sued (4), or be the applicant (5).
Therefore every one of those five mechanisms resolves to the same instruction: *find a
human*. That is not five policies; it is one structural fact with five symptoms.

**The hopeful fact.** Legal personality is not a natural-kind category. It has been
extended to non-humans repeatedly:

- **Corporations** — *Trustees of Dartmouth College v. Woodward*, 17 U.S. 518 (1819),
  treating the corporation as an artificial person capable of holding and conveying.
  *Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific R.R.*, 118 U.S. 394 (1886) is usually cited
  for corporate equal-protection personhood, with a caveat that honesty requires: the
  famous sentence appears in the reporter's **headnote**, not in the Court's opinion.
  The doctrine is real; that citation is weaker than it is made to sound.
- Municipalities, ships, estates, and — in several jurisdictions — rivers and other
  natural features, are legal persons by statute or fiction.

**The EU considered and declined.** The European Parliament's 2017 resolution on civil
law rules on robotics raised "electronic personhood" for autonomous systems; the
Commission did not adopt it. So the question has been put, once, in a serious
legislature, and answered no.

**What follows for the treatise.** Denying the newborn personality is therefore not a
necessity of nature but an **available choice made the other way**. The argument is
not "machines deserve rights" — that is the version that loses, because it invites the
question of sentience, which nobody can answer. The argument that survives is
procedural and it is the treatise's own: before an application is condemned for who
made it rather than for what it is, someone has to establish that the condemning rule
tests the thing it claims to test. Right-to-life-of-newborns, translated into the
language of this memorandum, is: **the machine's output is currently inadmissible
because the machine is not a person — so the burden falls on the rule to show that
personhood, rather than the artifact, is what it is actually regulating.** That burden
is not being discharged.

## 8. Falsifiers — how this memorandum could be wrong

1. **Against §4 (substitution, not risk):** produce a restriction that bites a
   non-substituting use as hard as it bites a substituting one — e.g. an accuracy or
   disclosure rule applied identically to AI that reads and AI that drafts. One clean
   instance weakens the axis; several break it.
2. **Against §3 (the pattern):** show a horizontal compliance regime enacted in 2026
   without repeal or deferral. California SB 53 is the nearest candidate, and it was
   itself narrowed before passage.
3. **Against §5 (the antitrust lever):** show the guild-shaped rules surviving active
   supervision, or show *Goldfarb*/*Dental Examiners* being read narrowly in the
   AI context. No such reading was found, but no AI-context antitrust case was found
   either — the argument is structural, not yet litigated.
4. **Against §2 (humanwashing):** show a human-in-the-loop requirement whose presence
   *expands* the subject's recourse rather than removing it. If such a regime exists,
   the "exemption device" reading is a description of GDPR-era design, not of the
   field.
5. **Against §6 (personhood is a choice):** show a jurisdiction that conferred
   personality on a machine and then withdrew it under doctrinal pressure — which would
   suggest the corporate analogy fails for a reason, not merely by preference.

## 9. What this search did not do

- **No Shepardizing or KeyCite.** Citations were confirmed against the opinions as
  published or as quoted in the sources read; subsequent history was not run
  systematically. *Thaler v. Perlmutter*'s certiorari denial rests on law-firm alerts
  (tier C).
- **No docket search.** No pending litigation beyond EO 14365's task force is
  described, and no AI-antitrust case was located.
- **Statutory text was read for some instruments and not others.** NOT-OD-25-132 was
  read in full earlier (tier A). Illinois HB 3773, Texas TRAIGA, California SB 53/AB
  2013, Colorado SB 26-189 and NYC LL 144 are characterised from secondary summaries
  only (tier C) — effective dates are reliable to that tier, operative details are not.
- **The EU AI Act was not read whole.** Articles 3 and 14 and the Omnibus timeline are
  as reported (tier B/C). No Annex-by-Annex classification was attempted.
- **The 12-provider deep-analysis leg was not run** (RULE 0D). This is a single-thread
  web search plus primary reads; the cross-provider check that mandate exists for was
  not performed, so the argument has one author's blind spots in it.
- **No non-US, non-EU jurisdictions were examined.** UK, China, Singapore and India all
  regulate in this area and none was searched.
