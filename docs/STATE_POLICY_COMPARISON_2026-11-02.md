# Which state has the better public policy, and why

Short answer: **no state wins on every dimension, and the honest ranking depends on which
dimension you weight. If the criterion is the floor — how well the worst-off actually do — the
answer is Minnesota.** Everything below is the reasoning, the runners-up, and the ways this kind
of comparison lies if you let it.

**Status.** Comparative judgement, not statistics. Social and economic figures below are
described at the level I can defend from widely reported public data (**tier C**); none is quoted
to a decimal, and any figure used officially needs pulling from the primary source for the year in
question. The criteria are mine and are stated so they can be argued with.

---

## 1. The criteria, because "better" is doing all the work

Any claim of "best state" is a claim about weights. I use six, in the order the Declaration
implies (equality first, consent second, rights before government):

1. **The floor** — health coverage, child poverty, life expectancy of the poorest quintile.
2. **Human capital** — school outcomes, including the gap between the best and worst districts.
3. **Security** — violent crime, and whether it is falling.
4. **Mobility** — the chance a child born poor ends up not poor.
5. **Democratic integrity** — registration, turnout, districting, access to records.
6. **Fiscal durability** — whether the promises survive the next recession without cuts to the floor.

Two traps to name up front. **Selection:** people move to states that fit them, so a state's
outcomes include who chose to live there. **Federal transfers:** every state is subsidised and
taxed by Washington at different rates, so "low tax" and "high service" are partly financed
elsewhere. Neither trap invalidates comparison; both cap how strong any conclusion can be.

## 2. Best in class, per criterion

| criterion | best in class | why it wins | the cost it pays |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **the floor** | **Minnesota**, then Massachusetts, Vermont | near-universal coverage, low child poverty for a high-income state, long life expectancy | high top marginal rates; a persistent racial achievement and income gap |
| **human capital** | **Massachusetts**, then New Jersey, Connecticut | strongest aggregate school outcomes; the 2006 coverage law was the template for the ACA | cost of living; segregation between districts |
| **security** | **New Hampshire**, **Maine**, **Vermont** | lowest violent-crime rates in the country for decades | small, homogeneous populations; the lesson does not transfer automatically |
| **mobility** | **Utah**, then Colorado, Minnesota | upward mobility is highest in the Mountain West and Upper Midwest — a durable finding in the Chetty-era research | cause is contested: two-parent stability and church community may matter more than any statute |
| **democratic integrity** | **Oregon** and **Washington** (mail voting, automatic registration), **Colorado** (same-day registration + mail) | the mechanics make participation the default | low turnout among the least-engaged persists anyway |
| **fiscal durability** | **Utah** | long-run management, rainy-day discipline, and a tax base that survives downturns | TABOR-style constraints elsewhere show the model can starve the floor |
| **environmental rights** | **Montana** (constitutional right, Art. | **labour and leave** | **New York**, **Washington**, **California** | paid family leave, high minimums, portable benefits experiments | small-business cost; benefit take-up gaps |

## 3. The pick, and the argument for it

**Minnesota**, on the floor criterion, for three reasons that are structural rather than
cultural:

1. **It buys outcomes rather than announcing them.** The state taxes progressively, spends on
   universal services, and ends up near the top on coverage, child poverty, life expectancy and
   school attainment simultaneously — the combination is the evidence, not any single number.
2. **It is durable across administrations.** The floor is institutional (a tax structure, a
   delivery system, a civic norm of participation), which is why it survives changes in who
   governs — the property that matters more than any one programme.
3. **It treats the state constitution as a working instrument.** Minnesota's constitution is
   amended routinely and its courts are not elected on partisan ballots, so rights arguments have
   somewhere to go — which is the mechanism §4 of the checks-and-balances document says is the
   country's most under-used correction.

**The counter-case, stated fairly, because it is the reason this is a judgement and not a
finding.** Minnesota's racial gaps in income, homeownership and school achievement are among the
worst in the country relative to its overall performance. A state that is excellent on the
average and poor at the gap has not met the Declaration's premise, which is *equality*, not
*aggregate prosperity*. On the Declaration standard the honest verdict is: **best floor in the
country, and not yet a fulfilled promise.** Massachusetts is the stronger claim on human capital
and health specifically; Oregon and Colorado are the stronger claim on consent; Utah on mobility
and fiscal durability.

---

## 4. The methodological warning that matters most

Rankings reward the wrong thing: they measure the *level* a state has reached, not the *distance
it moved* or the *cost it shifted onto people who cannot leave*. Three corrections I would insist
on before any of this is used to make policy:

- **Score the gap, not the average.** A state is only as good as its worst district, and every
  state publishes the data to compute that.
- **Score the trend, not the rank.** A state climbing from a low base with a coherent plan is
  doing better public policy than a state coasting on inherited advantage.
- **Score durability.** Ask of every programme: what happens to this in the next recession, and
  who is cut first? A floor that fails in a downturn was a ceiling with good publicity.

---

## 5. What this does not establish

It does not establish that Minnesota's policies *caused* its outcomes — the selection and culture
objections are real, and Utah's mobility is the clearest case where culture may dominate statute.
It does not rank all fifty states, and it does not use a composite index, because composite
indexes hide the weights that decide the answer. It is a defensible pick, with the criteria
exposed so the pick can be contested on the criteria rather than on vibes.
